Conservation Planning

Start with the resource concern — then figure out what fits the farm.

Conservation planning starts with understanding the farm, identifying the soil, water, plant, animal, or other resource concerns that need attention, and evaluating practical ways to address them.

KES works with farmers to understand what is happening, what they want to accomplish, and which conservation alternatives make sense for the operation.

…but you still have to farm.™

Resource Concerns on the Farm

What needs attention?

Resource concerns are the conditions conservation planning is intended to address.

Soil Loss

Sheet, rill, concentrated-flow, or wind erosion

Compaction

Restricted rooting, infiltration, or plant growth

Water Quality

Sediment, nutrients, manure, or other potential losses

Plant Condition

Cover, vigor, productivity, or suitability

Livestock Impacts

Heavy-use areas, access, traffic, or sensitive areas

Water Management

Drainage, runoff, concentrated flow, or safe conveyance

The purpose of planning is to understand what is causing the concern before deciding how to treat it.

Evaluating Alternatives The selected practices should address the concern and make sense for the operation.

KES works with the farmer to identify these and other resource concerns, understand what is contributing to them, and consider practical alternatives. Together, we evaluate the advantages, limitations, and trade-offs of each. The selected practices should address the concern and make sense for the operation.

You don't need to know which conservation practice you need before the planning starts. Start with what is happening on the farm and what you are trying to accomplish. The practice comes later.

Practical Conservation Planning Conservation concerns rarely exist in isolation.

KES helps farmers identify and understand resource concerns, evaluate alternatives, and develop conservation plans that fit the operation. Planning may involve several related parts of the farm when they contribute to the same concern, but the scope is driven by the conservation needs being addressed.

Grazing Management Grazing management connects livestock needs, forage production, and the condition of the land.

Planning may consider forage availability, livestock needs, grazing and recovery periods, water, fencing, sensitive areas, and livestock movement to develop a grazing system that works for both the animals and the land.

Cropland Conservation Planning Cropland planning connects production decisions with the conservation needs of the field.

Planning may consider erosion, soil condition, water movement, crop and residue management, tillage, field access, and other practices that protect the resource base while fitting the cropping system.
